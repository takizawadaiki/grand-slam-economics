Wimbledon’s prize fund has grown every decade since players were first paid in 1968. This page follows it through inflation, shows how the 2026 fund is divided between the champion and the first-round loser, and compares it with what the tournament earns.

05MethodSources and limits

Where the numbers come from

  • Prize money. The All England Club’s yearly announcements, as tabled on Wikipedia and on the Lawn Tennis Association’s site. There was no tournament in 2020.
  • Inflation. The Office for National Statistics consumer price index, which starts in 1988. Amounts are in 2025 pounds because 2026 is not yet complete.
  • The split. The 2026 prize for each round multiplied by the number of players who go out in it, for both singles draws.
  • Accounts. Turnover and profit of the company that runs the Championships, as reported from its filed accounts.
  • Other majors. Each tournament’s announced 2026 prize money.

What to keep in mind

  • Prize money is not income. Players pay for coaches, travel and tax out of it. This page does not estimate those costs.
  • Only two years of accounts. The share of turnover is shown for 2023 and 2024, the years I could source.
  • Turnover is not profit. Running the tournament costs most of what it earns. Nine-tenths of what is left goes to British tennis through the Lawn Tennis Association.
  • One tournament. The history is Wimbledon’s only. The other three majors are compared for 2026.
  • What was left out. An earlier version of this page showed combined Grand Slam revenue since 2000, players’ share of it, a player “impact score” and a career simulator. I could not trace those figures to sources, so they were removed.
